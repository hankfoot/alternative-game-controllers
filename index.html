    Alternative Game Controllers

    Originally designed by Avi Romanoff (@aviromanoff) and Hank Duhaime (@hankware.d)

    What if a pair of scissors, a stuffed animal or your own voice could control a video game? In this workshop, you’ll work in teams to build ridiculous new ways to play a classic game.

    Workshop slides ↗

    Gather your materials

    Everything you need before you start.

    1. 1

      Get a micro:bit

      You need a micro:bit V2 and a USB cable. A battery pack lets you unplug it from the computer later.

    2. 2

      Get to know the pins

      The gold strips along the bottom edge of the micro:bit are called pins. You connect things to them with alligator clips. The micro:bit reads from P0, P1 and P2. 3V supplies power, and GND (ground) is where the electricity returns.

      Never let a clip on 3V touch a clip on GND, or the micro:bit will restart.

    3. 3

      Collect things to build with

      Grab a few things from each group. Pick objects you can open, squeeze, swing or shout at. The stranger the better.

      🧲 Things that conduct electricity

      For buttons, switches and touch pads.

      • Aluminium foil or copper tape
      • Coins, keys, spoons or paper clips
      • Fruit and vegetables, like bananas
      • Play-Doh or a damp sponge
      • A thick pencil line on paper

      ✂️ Things to build with

      For holding it all together.

      • Alligator clips
      • Cardboard
      • Tape, string and rubber bands
      • Scissors
      • A hot glue gun

      🧸 Something to turn into a controller

      Anything you can move, open or make noise with.

      • A stuffed animal or a hat
      • A jar with a lid, or a box
      • A pair of scissors or tongs
      • A maraca, a cup of beans, a drum
      • A chair, a door or a drawer

    Set up your micro:bit

    1. 1

      Open MakeCode

      MakeCode is the website where you write code for the micro:bit. Open the starter project and click Edit Code in the top right. It starts empty, but Bluetooth is already turned on, so always start from this project.

    2. 2

      Copy the starter code

      Click JavaScript at the top of the editor and paste in this code (⌘ V, or Ctrl V on Windows). It turns on Bluetooth and uses the lights to show whether the micro:bit is connected.

    3. 3

      Upload the code

      Plug the micro:bit into your computer and click Download in the bottom left of MakeCode. The first time, follow the steps to pair it.

      On a Mac, you may see “Allow accessory to connect?” when you plug it in. Click Allow.

      A light on the back blinks while the code copies, and stops when it’s done.

    4. 4

      Connect to your micro:bit

      While it waits to connect, the micro:bit shows an X, a Bluetooth symbol and its five-letter name. Every micro:bit has its own name, so note yours.

      Click Connect at the top of this page, choose BBC micro:bit with your micro:bit’s name, and click Pair. The micro:bit shows a smiley face when it connects.

      You’ll use this Connect button for every example. If you reload the page, connect again.

    5. +

      Go cordless Optional

      The code stays on the micro:bit after you unplug it. Plug in a battery pack instead of the USB cable to move around freely.

    Code it

    Want something these controls can’t do, like flapping every third clap? Ask an AI chatbot to write the code.

    1. 1

      Describe what you want

      Write one sentence about what should happen, then click the button. It copies your sentence with instructions for the AI. Paste it into any chatbot.

    2. 2

      Add the code to MakeCode

      In MakeCode’s JavaScript tab, paste the AI’s code at the very bottom and click Download. Wire it up the way the AI’s Hookup guide says. If MakeCode shows a red error, paste it back into the chatbot and ask it to fix it.
